## A Quebec company is taking milkweed into the Den on September 17

### Lasclay turns the weed that monarch caterpillars depend on into winter insulation. It appears in the season 21 premiere of *Dragons' Den*.

**Quebec City, September 2, 2026.** Lasclay, a Quebec company that turns milkweed floss into
textile insulation, appears in the first episode of season 21 of *Dragons' Den*, airing Thursday,
September 17, 2026 at 8 p.m. (8:30 NT) on CBC and CBC Gem.

Founder Gabriel Gouveia pitches a plant most Canadians know as a roadside nuisance. Milkweed is
the only plant monarch caterpillars can eat, and the silky floss attached to its seeds is hollow,
extremely light, and naturally water repellent. Lasclay uses it as insulation in mittens, coats,
toques, lunch bags and soft coolers.

We cannot say anything about how the pitch went until it airs.

### An industry that already failed once

Quebec had a first wave of milkweed enthusiasm around 2013 and 2014. Farmers put fields into
production, a textile supply chain was announced, and most of it collapsed by 2018. Harvesting was
harder than expected, processing was not solved, and commercial outlets did not arrive fast enough.
Growers plowed fields under or switched crops.

Lasclay started in 2020, after that collapse, with one product: mittens insulated with milkweed
floss. The catalogue now runs to more than 40 products, and the company has distributed roughly
10 million milkweed seeds across North America through its national planting campaign, now in its
fifth year. About a fifth of recent revenue comes from the United States, mostly from the
horticultural side.

### What is made where

Milkweed growing, floss-to-insulation processing and product design happen in Quebec, in a
workshop in the Limoilou district of Quebec City. Final textile assembly for most finished
products is done externally, largely in Tunisia, following a manufacturing shift the company
announced publicly in 2025. That trade-off is what brought a milkweed-insulated coat to market at
around $300, after earlier industry attempts priced above $500 and $1,000 and were all
discontinued.

### Why the plant matters

The monarch butterfly is listed as endangered in Canada under the Species at Risk Act, a status
confirmed in 2024. In the United States, the Fish and Wildlife Service proposed listing it as
threatened in late 2024. The decline is largely attributed to milkweed disappearing from
agricultural land.

Lasclay's argument is economic before it is environmental: if milkweed pays, farmers have a reason
to keep it in the ground, and monarchs get breeding habitat back. The link is systemic, not
magical. Buying one product does not save one butterfly.

### About Lasclay

Founded in 2020 and based in Quebec City, Lasclay develops products, processes and manufacturing
technology around milkweed, with the goal of making the crop profitable enough that monarch
habitat comes back with it. Products are sold online at lasclay.com in French and English, across
Canada and the United States.
